Reykjavík's climate shapes the whole project: strong winds from many directions, up to 27 m/s, and daylight that swings from long summer days to very short winter ones. The system has to adapt, protect and still create comfortable public space.

The form draws on Iceland's basalt columns and on turf houses. The building is a kit of parts derived from the quasi-brick, a twelve-sided polyhedron, rationalised into prisms that stack without gaps. Five module families (core, public, semi-private, support and event) aggregate under rules that give every module access through a core or a public space.

A topological graph on the site places the cores; k-means clustering zones larger sites, and evolutionary optimisation picks the footprint with the best pedestrian wind comfort and built area. The aggregation then grows floor by floor, followed by openings, interior functions, a hexagonal facade clustered into four panel types, and structural analysis.
